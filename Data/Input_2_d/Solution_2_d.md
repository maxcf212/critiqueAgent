### Executive Summary

This report outlines the intended designs and procedures involved in the construction of a Earth observation satellite – specifically for coral reef and ocean monitoring. The satellite must provide images for a fluid lensing tool to analyse reefs beneath the water surface. It must therefore be placed in an orbit with optimal lighting and viewing angles, with a goal of maximising the contact period between the proposed satellite and a ground station. Key features of this satellite configuration include a direct launch into a retrograde sun-synchronous polar orbit, whose exact Keplerian elements are selected for a low altitude and constant sun angle. The satellite, nicknamed ZYZZsat (Z-axis/Y-axis Zeroed Zoning Satellite), is operated and accessed from Australia ground facilities, and is designed specifically for use above the Great Barrier Reef. This document also includes the explored processes involved in designing each mission segment, including Systems Tool Kit (STK) simulations, perturbation calculations, launch mission alternatives evaluation, and analysis for end-of-life procedure.

## Introduction

Coral reefs harbour some of the most diverse species of life on the planet, but this is not even the full extent of their nurture. Hundreds of millions of humans rely on reefs for food, natural protection, jobs, and tourism. Over the last century, human-induced environmental change – as a result of increased burning of fossil fuels and deforestation – has put huge ecosystems at risk. Oceans have become 30 percent more acidic since the industrial revolution leading to the bleaching of coral reefs [1]. These bleaching events are destructive enough to wipe out entire reefs, displacing billions of animals in the process.

Australia’s Great Barrier Reef is the largest coral reef in the world, spread over 344,400 km². It is one of the most complex, vast, and flourishing ecosystems on Earth, but it is due to suffer several more bleaching events in the coming years. In 2016, a bleaching killed 30 percent of the reef’s coral. In 2017, another 20 percent perished [2].

Coral reefs, specifically the Great Barrier Reef, would immensely benefit from dedicated satellite monitoring. A near-real-time relay to monitoring stations on the ground would allow marine and conservation scientists to more accurately predict and react to coral bleaching events. Reef-monitoring satellites could also be used to report sea surface temperatures, thermal stress on coral, and bathymetry (current satellites have only mapped Great Barrier Reef depths to a resolution of 100 meters per pixel) [3].

Traditionally, coral bleaching was monitored by plane fly-by surveying, and then more recently by satellite temperature monitoring. However, while high temperatures can lead to coral bleaching, it is not a direct indicator that bleaching is occurring (or at what severity). A newer technology called fluid lensing has been used in combination with AI analysis to detect colour/tone changes to the seafloor, where lighter hues indicate bleaching [4]. This tool is what will be carried up and utilised by the proposed satellite ‘ZYZZsat’.

The spacecraft is intended to image and send data to ground stations for collation and analysis. To do this, a number of segments must be evaluated and compiled into one mission. These stages include launch, orbit, ground control and communications during said orbit, and the end-of-life procedure.

Other Earth observation satellites use networks or constellations of satellites (i.e. multiple satellites spaced out in the same orbit). The ZYZZsat however, does not require such constant

coverage as the reef bleaching events occur over days and weeks. Thus, only one satellite is required. This also just allows for simplicity in communications, reduces cost, and requires less time to build and launch.

Firstly, a launch vehicle and location must be determined. The proposed satellite is small enough that a separate rocket is not required, and it should instead partake in the common practice of ‘piggybacking’. Therefore, the search is for the most appropriate pre-planned mission, which will be dependent on a number of factors including the original – vehicle and location. There are already several satellite missions every month, with future plans that look just as promising.

The most efficient rocket launches are from locations close to the equator as this is where maximum rotational velocity can be experienced [5]. This in turn, requires less fuel – saving money and more importantly – weight. The launch location is also dependent on the country, as factors like trade, politics, etc. come into play. It would be significantly easier to co-ordinate launches as well as build a satellite in closely allied countries (i.e. not Russia or China). An exponentially growing commercial space sector in Europe and the USA specifically, will lead to relatively cheaper manufacturing and launch costs.

To compare existing spaceflight mission plans, a wiki sorted directory – ‘Timeline of spaceflight’ – will be referred to throughout the report [6].

The actual launch service provider is important as some providers will only launch their own countries' satellites or even specific companies. The process of launching the proposed satellite will be easier with a larger, more experienced provider – e.g. SpaceX, Arianespace, ULA. Regulatory standards will also be more thorough in locations like the US and Europe.

The launch vehicle chosen will already be capable of transporting small satellites into the optimal orbit. The problem then is to secure a space on one of these missions for the proposed satellite. Weight should also be minimised to reduce launch cost.

Launch vehicles typically inject their passenger satellites into the same orbit. Thus, the other satellites of the chosen launch mission should have the same eventual desired orbit as the proposed satellite. This is calculated and explained in detail in the orbit section of the report.

The proposed satellite would likely take at least four years to be launch-ready, and so launch missions after 2025 will be considered viable. Current spaceflight missions this far away are only very generally planned and do not go into much detail or consider piggyback satellites as they have not been decided upon yet. However, the spaceflight calendar for February 2021 displays two promising examples of systems whose specifics fit the proposed satellite, and that could be replicated in the future.

The first launch alternative is Arianespace's Vega Flight VV18 – a small satellites mission service. It's satellite 'passengers' are set to enter a desired low-earth sun-synchronous orbit and consist of various functions, including Earth observation – the main function of the proposed satellite. The second launch alternative is a similar rideshare mission via SpaceX's Falcon 9 Block 5 – currently planned to carry up a selection of technology demonstration satellites.

The Vega VV flights launch from the Guiana Space Centre’s Spaceport, in Kourou, French Guiana. This location is ideal as the surrounding area is vast and empty, as well as the fact that it is situated very close to the equator, with a latitude of $ 45.05^{\circ} $N. Orbit

Underwater imaging by satellite already proves difficult after just a few metres of water, as light is distorted by inconsistencies and waves – an effect which intensifies exponentially with depth [7]. It is therefore important that the proposed satellite maintain a consistent view of the observation location. This allows for an easy comparison of images, as variables like lighting and viewing angle are kept as similar as possible. The most suitable orbit for this task would be a low-earth sun-synchronous orbit.

Sun-synchronous orbit means the satellite passes over any given latitude at the same local mean solar time each day, providing a uniform illumination of the area over time. This is why many Earth observation and weather satellites also use sun-synchronous orbits, including those in the VV18 SSMS flight.

The orbit is near-polar and retrograde, meaning the satellite will pass over almost the entire Earth – with a frequent orbital period. This is beneficial for future endeavours should the satellite’s mission be expanded to monitor other coral reefs besides the Great Barrier Reef. Additionally, the constant supply of sunlight to the satellite will be captured by solar panels and used to power on-board electronics.

Selecting an appropriate altitude is dependent on two opposing factors – swath width and ground resolution. The satellite must be far away enough to capture the width of the Great

Barrier Reef – if daily images are to capture the entire reef – and low enough that image resolution is of sufficient quality for analysis. Alternatively, the satellite may adapt a lower altitude and cover only a section of the reef each orbit. Images could then be compiled to form an accurate, but delayed view.

The widest portion of the Great Barrier Reef measures roughly 250km. The swath required for the satellite to cover the reef’s entirety would require either the sensor to swing or a very high altitude. For reef imaging, the sensor should be as directly overhead the reef as possible, so a swinging sensor could distort accuracy. A very high altitude would yield low resolution images which is also desirable. Thus, the satellite will be positioned at an altitude of 600km above Earth’s surface and images of reef sections will be complied daily.

Specifically, an altitude of 600km requires a mean inclination of $ 87.7924^{\circ} $ for the satellite to remain sun-synchronous.

We typically describe an orbit using six ‘classic orbital elements’, but for some particular orbits (e.g. circular, equatorial), these elements are undefined – meaning a full description of the orbit requires additional information. The orbit of the proposed satellite is circular and thus has no perigee. This means that two of the six classical orbit elements – argument of perigee and true anomaly – are undefined.

Instead, an ‘alternative orbital element’ is introduced to provide information equivalent to that of the undefined elements – the ‘Argument of Latitude.’ This element describes the angle from the ascending node to the spacecraft’s position vector.

The sun-synchronous orbit inherently experiences significant J2 perturbations as a result of its highly polar inclination. This is what allows the orbit to maintain its orientation relative to the Sun. For the orbit to be truly sun-synchronous, RAAN must change at a rate of $ 360^{\circ} $ per year (i.e. a full rotation); the ascending node experiencing a $ 1^{\circ} $ east-ward precession every day [8]. Keplerian elements can now be used to fully describe the orbit of the proposed satellite.

Ground Control & Communications

A ground station allows for satellite-collected data to be received periodically throughout the satellites orbits – when in range of the facility. It is essential that the satellite actually passes over the ground facility’s sensor for data to be transferred. The more passes it makes, the more up-to-date the data is. To maximise the frequency of passes that occur between the satellite and ground, a ground station is selected whose latitude will be frequented by the satellite. In the case of a near-polar sun-synchronous orbit, access frequency is maximised by selecting a ground facility close to the poles [9].

In addition to passes being of high frequency, long contact durations are also ideal. These are ensured by the proposed satellite's orbital inclination, which – because of its verticality – maintains a significant contact for the vast majority of passes. In the case of the proposed satellite, the ground facility will be able to receive all new data from the previous period, as well as examine data in real-time for contact duration.

Another factor in selecting an appropriate ground station is ease of setup and maintenance. A ground station inside the country from which the satellite is operated offers a number of benefits. It reduces the amount of correspondence between monitoring personnel and analysts as compared to requesting data from another country; laws, regulations, and set-up costs will be more manageable as the government shares interest; funding and other activities in favour of the Great Barrier Reef's preservation will favour Australian companies and facilities.

As pass frequency is already substantial enough for the purposed of this satellite, a ground facility within Australia will suffice. As explained, a more polar latitude will yield higher pass frequency, and so a Tasmanian facility is selected – the Mount Pleasant Radio Observatory). This station is current owned and operated by the University of Tasmania and features a large array of receivers for each of two radio telescopes.

Mission Duration & EOL

The mission should cover a large enough total period such that clear environmental change can be recognised upon image analysis. Bleaching events, however, are measured over years and so a replacement satellite will likely be required after the current EOL. A new satellite will also mean that new technology can be sent up with it. This is beneficial as fluid lensing technology is relatively new and should see breakthroughs and improvement for decades to come.

A span of five years is a sound estimate of the useful lifespan of the proposed satellite. This may be lengthened though, by the addition of solar panels for electrical power. STK simulations have been run from Feb 3, 2025 to Feb 3, 2030.

An end-of-life strategy should be implemented for the satellite so as to minimise space debris left in orbit. Sun-synchronous orbits are particularly densely populated by space debris – a problem as this is also a highly useful orbit, as well as that of the proposed satellite. Thus, abandoning the satellite at its EOL is not environmentally or socially responsible.

We will consider two functional strategies of the disposal of the proposed satellite. One option is that the satellite is manoeuvred further out into a ‘graveyard orbit’, where it remains – unobstructive of future satellites and space missions. This process is commonly applied to satellites in geo-synchronous orbits – as they are already high altitude – and requires a small amount of propulsion (i.e. fuel) to perform the orbital transfer. The second option is to de-orbit the satellite. For different satellites, de-orbiting may be controlled – wherein the satellite undergoes retro-burn propulsion directed towards a final ground impact zone – or uncontrolled – involving a less complex deceleration of the satellite into a lower orbit. In this orbit, atmospheric drag should cause the satellite to completely burn up upon re-entry.

The proposed ZYZZsat is small and without thrusters in a low-earth orbit. It would therefore make sense to choose an uncontrolled descent as an end-of-life strategy, since it requires the least amount of energy to perform. However, the ZYZZsat is without thrust capability and so must use a ‘passive’ strategy.

The most promising alternative is a ‘drag sail’ – a drag-enhancing deorbit device that deploys at the EOL. It acts like a regular sail, utilising a large surface area to ‘ and is suitable for low-earth orbit altitudes [10].

## Conclusion

After analysis of each mission segment, a fully described satellite configuration has been constructed. The initial problem of not catching coral bleaching events in time can be minimised, if not solved, by the initiation of the ZYZZsat in 2025. A specific launch vehicle – the Vega E – has been selected although it has not been built yet. Thus, launch may need to be adjusted if the rocket is still not constructed by the proposed date. Alternatives include the earlier Vega C launcher. A polar sun-synchronous orbit allows for consistent illumination of the oceans while maintaining a constant sun angle. The Mt Pleasant Radio Observatory is well-located to receive data from this orbit type constantly and for long durations. This will maximise the usefulness of the data and hopefully contribute to the prevention of coral bleaching within the Great Barrier Reef and elsewhere.
