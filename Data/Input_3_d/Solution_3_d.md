# ETHICS REPORT: To Ship or Not to Ship

#### INTRODUCTION

Ethics are a critical component to a functioning society. In the context of engineering design, a rigorous ethical framework is paramount.

In the given scenario, Rachel is a Quality Assurance Engineer at a large electronics company faced with an ethical dilemma — that being whether she can morally justify shipping potentially defective products. This decision is a microcosm of one of the most common issues for engineers and engineering organisations in a rapidly growing technology industry: the issue of maintaining product quality safety while trying to succeed as a business in a competitive field.

The impact of an individual decision like Rachel’s can have a widespread impact on many parties. The relevant stakeholders in this scenario include Rachel, Rachel’s company, the chip contract company, the government health department, and the many people who will use, be affected by, or rely on the services of the government health department. The potential harm to end-users and responsibility of Rachel and the company are key considerations in this decision.

It is important to consider that there may not be a black-and-white ethical solution for Rachel, but she must weigh both sides and choose the most ethical path.

### DICUSSION: To Ship or Not to Ship

Arguments for Rachel shipping potentially defective servers are as follows.

Firstly, shipping the servers on time would give Rachel’s company a competitive advantage in the market. If the two-year product release cycle is maintained, the company has the best chance of continuing to grow. Apple Inc. has demonstrated this competitive advantage best through its strategy of regular product releases. This approach meets consumer demand for innovation while outpacing large competitors like Samsung and Google [1].

Secondly, securing a high-value contract with a government health department could lead to significant revenue for Rachel’s company and job security for Rachel and other employees. The economic benefits of could very well be the difference between the company surviving in the long term or being outpaced by a competitor with more resources. This begs the question: if Rachel decides not to ship the servers, what’s to say one of her competitors won’t take the deal and ship out potentially defective products themselves. If this happened, Rachel might

lose her job, have her company might go under, and end-users would still have to deal with the consequences of defective products.

Thirdly, shipping the servers on time could allow Rachel and her company to grow large enough temporarily to improve the speed of the design and manufacturing process so that a 6- month window for complete quality assurance checks is actually viable. In this case, the company may suffer a loss of reputation if defects were discovered by end-users, but it would survive. The company could then go on to create better products, with new innovations, which might provide a great benefit to end-users and society at large.

Now, examining arguments against Rachel shipping potentially defective servers.

Perhaps the most important issue in Rachel’s dilemma is the risk of harm to end-users. While Rachel might ship a product that malfunctions and directly causes harm to an end-user, the potential loss of data, especially in a healthcare setting, defective servers could lead to disruptions in emergency services and patient care in hospitals. If even 1 percent of the 5,000 servers in the contract failed, that would be 500 failed servers in hospitals. Another perspective can be seen in Rachel’s professional responsibility. Engineers following a code of ethics such as those provided by Engineers Australia (EACE), have a duty to prioritise public safety above all else [2]. Shipping potentially defective servers will not only violate these standards, but also erode trust that society has in Rachel’s company, and the engineering industry.

The monetary incentive of pushing out the potentially defective servers on time may sustain Rachel and her company temporarily. However, the long-term consequences of deteriorated brand loyalty, hefty fines, settlements, and legal fees, may well be enough to crumble Rachel’s company, and thus her job too. In this case the relevant stakeholders - Rachel, Rachel’s company, the chip contract company, the government health department, and all the people in society negatively affected by the server’s defects - would all lose.

##### The Most Ethical Path

As seen by the points raised in the discussion, it is clear that Rachel faces a difficult decision. While there is some merit to shipping the products, such as economic benefits and maintaining a competitive advantage, the short-term benefit trade-off is greatly outweighed by the potential risks of harm to end-users, erosion ofprofessional integrity, and long-term reputational damage to Rachel, the two companies, and the government health department.

The severity of potential harm to end-users cannot be understated. In late 2023, the Health Services Safety Investigations Body (HSSIB) investigated a series ofIT system failures in NHS hospitals across England. This resulted in several hundred thousand hospital letters being lost and led to catastrophic consequences: doctors not being able to operate because they could not access medical records; a patient who was given incorrect medication due to a mix-up in electronic notes and died eighteen days later; a patient who was not notified that he had been diagnosed with cancer [3]. While data loss is always bad, the impacts it can have in the healthcare sector are unparalleled. Rachel may think she doesn’t ship products with defects that can cause physical harm, but as demonstrated in the case above, an almost identical malfunction scenario — even if indirect — led to deaths.

Furthermore, if that wasn’t reason enough, Rachel must also consider her professional integrity and societal duty as an engineer. Adhering to ethical frameworks such as the EACE is essential for the very existence of engineering as an industry. Shipping out potentially defective servers violates several guidelines in the EACE [2], specifically:

- © 2.1b—Actin a careful and diligent manner The short six-month product cycle is, by Rachel’s admission, not long enough to perform proper rigorous QA testing — i.e. diligence.

- e 2.3b - Inform employers or clients if a task requires qualifications and experience outside your areas ofcompetence
  - Rachel’s company should have informed its clients that with its current resources, proper testing cannot be done within a six-month period.

- e 3.3 — Make reasonable efforts to communicate honestly and effectively to all stakeholders, taking into account the reliance of others on engineering expertise Unless Rachel’s company plans to secure the government contract with the health department being fully aware of potential defects, it is assumed that this information

would not be communicated — meaning knowingly withholding the potential risks from the health department and as a result, end-users too.

By refusing to ship, Rachel would demonstrate her professional integrity and commitment to public safety in the face of the conflict of immediate business interests.

Finally, building and maintaining trust and reputation is the key to a successful business. If consumers lose trust and brand reputation is damaged, clients will opt for Rachel’s competitors instead. In the case of the Volkswagen emissions scandal, it was discovered that Volkswagen had installed “defeat devices” in a large amount of its electric vehicles worldwide to manipulate crash-safety tests [4]. The scandal made a massive detrimental impact to Volkswagen’s reputation, with a significant loss of consumer trust, in addition to billions of dollars in fines and legal fees. Even through a purely monetary lens, the long-term consequences of shipping defective servers outweigh the short-term revenue from securing one contract with the health department. Shipping potentially defective products is not an ethical way to conduct business.

#### CONCLUSION

Rachel’s decision to ship or not ship exemplifies a universal issue in today’s competitive world of production — the struggle to meet business needs while maintaining ethical standards.

While shipping may result in short-term revenue gains and a competitive advantage, the tradeoff risks to public safety, integrity of engineers and the engineering industry, and business reputation are colossal in comparison. The potential harm to end-users, particularly in healthcare, highlights how important Rachel’s choice really is. As explained, it could be the difference between life and death for some. Additionally, following ethical standards is necessary for the growth of engineering as an industry, but also more personally for Rachel — to do the morally right thing. Finally, from a business perspective, being responsible for a scandal and having to pay fines and legal fees could just as easily lead to Rachel’s company going under as it may if it had simply been outpaced in the market.

Rachel’s dilemma may be difficult personally, but in the full context of all relevant stakeholders - the decision to Not Ship is certainly the most ethical path.

### RECOMMENDATION

1. Rachel should accept the contract with the government health department.

2. Rachel should firmly raise the issue of the quality assurance window being too short to her company.

3. Rachel’s company should delay their launches or switch to a longer product cycle until they have the resources to achieve a six-month product cycle.

4. Rachel’s company should notify current and future clients of the ongoing timing issues, and transparently explain why launches must be delayed.
